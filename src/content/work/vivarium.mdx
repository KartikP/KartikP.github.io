## Context

<Section id="context" eyebrow="CONTEXT" heading="A rodent colony is mostly a calendar problem.">

A behavioural-neuroscience lab running a rodent colony spends an enormous
amount of attention on dates. Every breeding pair sets off a chain of
obligations: a plug check the next morning, a birth prediction nineteen
days later, sexing a few weeks after that, weaning at postnatal day 17, a
sequence of neonatal protocols anchored to each litter's birth, and a
sequence of adolescent protocols anchored to the cohort's median birth date
plus an offset that's different for males and females.

Most labs manage this with a whiteboard, a shared spreadsheet, and a
combination of memory and post-its. The spreadsheet drifts. The
post-its fall off. A missed weaning day cascades into a missed
behavioural session two weeks later, which means the cohort gets dropped
from analysis. The cost of a single missed date can be a month of work.

</Section>

## The premise

<Section id="premise" eyebrow="PREMISE" heading="Every date worth tracking is derivable from a small number of inputs.">

The observation Vivarium is built around: of all the dates a researcher
tracks for a colony, **almost none of them are independent**. They cascade
out of a handful of inputs:

- A pairing window
- A confirmed birth date
- A protocol definition (with offsets in days, optionally split by sex)

If you write down the inputs once and the protocols once, every concrete
event — for every pup, in every litter, in every cohort, on every day for
the next three months — is a deterministic function of those inputs.
Researchers shouldn't be entering individual events. They should be
declaring inputs and watching events appear.

<Callout variant="goal" eyebrow="THE GOAL">
Move the researcher's job from "track every date manually" to "confirm
the predictions the system makes." The calendar should be a side effect
of correctly entered colony data, not a parallel artefact.
</Callout>

</Section>

## Data flow

<Section id="data-flow" eyebrow="DATA FLOW" heading="Pairings cascade into litters, litters cascade into events.">

The shape of the model mirrors the shape of the colony. A pairing produces
a litter. A litter produces pups. Protocol steps materialise into events
on specific dates per pup or per cage.

The key invariant: **events are computed, never entered**. Editing a
litter's birth date doesn't ask the researcher to also edit fifty
downstream events; the events update because they were never standalone.

## Sex-staggered protocols

<Section id="sex-staggered" eyebrow="SUBTLETY THAT MATTERS" heading="One step, two timelines.">

A behaviourist running adolescent protocols often wants to test males and
females on consecutive days — males at P30, females at P31 — to keep
session loads even and avoid pseudo-replication.

In a typical scheduling tool, that means duplicating every step: one row
for males at offset 30, one row for females at offset 31. Forty steps in
the protocol becomes eighty rows; any change has to happen twice; the
two rows drift apart over time.

<Callout variant="design" eyebrow="DESIGN CONSIDERATION">
A protocol step should be declared once, with the female offset declared
as a *property of the step*, not as a separate step. The materialiser
handles the day-shift on its own.
</Callout>

The model lets a step define `day_offset` and an optional
`female_offset_days`. When events are materialised, the system splits
each subject by sex and emits one event per group on the correct day.
The protocol stays one row; the calendar gets two events.

</Section>

## The calendar feed

<Section id="calendar-feed" eyebrow="DELIVERY" heading="The interface is a calendar subscription.">

Researchers do not want to learn a new app to check what's happening
today. They already live in Google Calendar or Apple Calendar. The
question Vivarium had to answer: *how does the data get there?*

The answer was an **ICS feed**, served at a per-user tokenised URL like
`/api/feed/<token>.ics`. The user adds it once as a subscribed calendar in
Google or Apple. From then on, every event Vivarium materialises shows up
on their phone, on their laptop, in their meeting reminders, with no extra
software installed and nothing to check.

## Stack and conventions

<Section id="stack" eyebrow="BUILD" heading="A small system with strong rules.">

The stack is unsurprising on purpose: Postgres for the data, FastAPI on
the server, Next.js on the client, Docker Compose to run the whole thing
behind a Cloudflare Tunnel on a homelab box. The constraints that *do*
matter aren't visible in a list of dependencies — they're enforced as
project conventions.

<Callout variant="pick" eyebrow="THE THREE RULES">
**Every schema change goes through an Alembic migration.** The database
is never touched by hand, even in development. The migration history is
the schema's documentation. <br/><br/>
**Every domain table carries `user_id`.** The current Vivarium runs for
one researcher, but the schema is multi-tenant from day one. Migrating
to a hosted SaaS will be a deployment change, not a schema rewrite. <br/><br/>
**The CLI owns user creation and feed-token rotation.** Credentials and
tokens never live in source. The first user is bootstrapped from
the command line during deployment.
</Callout>

</Section>

## What's next

<Section id="next" eyebrow="WHAT'S NEXT" heading="Open questions and obvious extensions.">

Vivarium runs for one researcher today. The interesting next questions
are operational rather than architectural:

- **Cage capacity awareness.** The materialiser currently emits events
  blind to whether a cage is full. The natural extension is a soft
  warning when a weaning event would exceed cage capacity, with a
  suggested split.
- **Behavioural-session conflict detection.** Two cohorts whose
  adolescent windows overlap can collide on a behaviour rig. The data is
  all there — the system just doesn't yet flag the collision.
- **Multi-user lab mode.** The `user_id` foreign keys are in place; the
  remaining work is permissioning, role definition (PI / postdoc /
  technician), and shared cohort visibility.

The core observation — that almost every date worth tracking is
derivable, not declared — has held up well. The bigger Vivarium gets, the
more weight it puts on that idea.

</Section>
