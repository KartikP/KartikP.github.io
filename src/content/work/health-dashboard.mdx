## Why I built this

<Section id="motivation" eyebrow="MOTIVATION" heading="Six apps, one body, no overlap.">

My health data lives in six places — Oura, Strava, Apple Health, a Stelo
CGM, Function Health, and a notebook of supplements I keep forgetting
to update. None of them talk to each other. None of them can answer
the question I actually want to ask, which is: *did this supplement
move my deep sleep, or am I just imagining it?* The cross-source
correlations that make the data useful are exactly the ones no
commercial app will ever build, because they're n=1 by design.

I also didn't want my health data living in someone else's database.
That's a strong opinion, and it's the source of most of the
self-hosting tax this project pays — the tunnel, the watchdog, the
upgrade cadence, the backup story. I think it's worth it.

The third reason is more honest: I wanted an excuse to build something
in 3D.

</Section>

## What it does today

<Section id="status" eyebrow="STATUS" heading="Working today, with caveats.">

Five data sources sync on schedule. Oura and Strava run on an hourly
cron in the homelab; Apple Health (and a Stelo CGM piggybacking on it)
arrives via a 30-minute webhook from the *Auto Health Export* iOS
app; lab panels are entered manually after each draw. The Apple
Health pipeline alone has ingested 2.1M+ historical rows.

## The 3D anatomy pipeline

<Section id="anatomy-pipeline" eyebrow="WAR STORY 01" heading="From a 293 MB Blender file to 6.9 MB of holographic edges.">

The "holographic" 3D body view is the thing visitors notice first, and
it's almost entirely a rendering trick. There's no proprietary
anatomy asset behind it — the source is *Z-Anatomy*, an open-source
Blender file with 4,569 individually named meshes, weighing in at
293 MB. Unusable as-is on the web.

The pipeline that turns it into a 6.9 MB GLB is short, and most of the
work happens before Three.js sees a single triangle:

<KeyFlow
  num={1}
  title="Curate in Blender"
  image="/assets/work/health-dashboard/anatomy-source.png"
  imageAlt="Z-Anatomy source file — thousands of individual meshes in Blender"
>
A Python script walks the source file and extracts 281 target meshes —
the named bones, the visceral organs, the brain regions I care about.
The 131 individual brain parts get merged into a single mesh up front
because nobody's clicking on the cerebellum.
</KeyFlow>

<KeyFlow
  num={2}
  title="Decimate via gltf-transform"
  image="/assets/work/health-dashboard/anatomy-decimate.png"
  imageAlt="Decimation pass reducing vertex count from 581k to 145k"
>
The exported GLB enters at 581k vertices and leaves at 145k after a
quality-aware decimation pass. The simplification preserves silhouettes,
which is all the holographic style cares about.
</KeyFlow>

<KeyFlow
  num={3}
  title="Render only edges"
  image="/assets/work/health-dashboard/anatomy-edges.png"
  imageAlt="Final render — wireframe edges with additive blending and bloom against a dark background"
>
In Three.js, every mesh is wrapped in an `EdgesGeometry` with additive
blending and a bloom postprocess pass. The fill is invisible. That's
the entire "holographic" effect — the look is just edges + bloom + a
navy background.
</KeyFlow>

<KeyFlow
  num={4}
  title="Collapse on the GPU"
  image="/assets/work/health-dashboard/anatomy-merged.png"
  imageAlt="Three.js scene graph showing 568 meshes merged into ~20 buffers"
>
`mergeGeometries()` compresses the 568 remaining draw calls into ~20
buffers grouped by region. With `frameloop="demand"` and in-place
`setRGB()` colour mutations, GPU usage sits at zero when the camera
isn't moving.
</KeyFlow>

<Video
  src="/assets/work/health-dashboard/anatomy-final.mp4"
  caption="The final view, recorded live. Region tabs change which buffer is highlighted; double-clicking on a body part zooms the camera."
  framed={false}
/>

The lesson is the boring one: most "performant 3D on the web" is just
not asking the browser to do work it doesn't have to. Edges instead of
fills. Merged geometry instead of 568 draw calls. Skip the render
loop when the camera is still. The 145k vertex count is incidental.

</Section>

## Multi-source step deduplication

<Section id="dedup" eyebrow="WAR STORY 02" heading="30,000-step days I didn't actually walk.">

Both Oura and Apple Health write to the same `steps` table, tagged by
source. They report in completely different shapes. Oura returns one
total per day. Apple Health writes per-second granular increments
because Apple Watch is recording them as I move. A naive
`SUM(count)` across both gives me 30,000-step days I definitely did
not walk — Oura's daily roll-up is being added on top of Apple's
per-minute deltas that *already sum to the same number*.

The fix is shaped the way most multi-source aggregation problems are:

<Callout>
Bucket per `(day, source)`. Aggregate within source according to
the source's actual shape — `MAX` for Oura (one daily roll-up
already), `SUM` for Apple (genuinely additive minute-level data).
Then pick the best source per day. Don't sum *across* sources.
</Callout>

<Figure
  src="/assets/work/health-dashboard/steps-chart.png"
  alt="Daily steps chart with All/Oura/Apple Watch toggle showing best-source-per-day"
  caption="The dashboard's steps chart with the three-way source filter. Each day shows the best source for that day, not a sum across sources."
/>

The same exact-shape problem hits heart rate, HRV, and SpO₂. Both
Oura and Apple Watch write to `heart_rate`. The dashboard chart
overlays them in different shades of red on a minute-bucketed
x-axis, with a three-way *All / Oura / Apple Watch* filter that
re-keys the EKG sweep, the legend, and the bpm range together. It's
five lines of UI built on top of a deduplication rule the database
enforces.

<Figure
  src="/assets/work/health-dashboard/hr-overlay.png"
  alt="Heart rate chart with Oura and Apple Watch sources overlaid, EKG sweep dot tracing the active source"
  caption="Heart rate with both sources overlaid. The animated EKG dot picks the closest sample to its previous position so it doesn't jump between Oura and Apple Watch lines as it sweeps."
/>

The other quiet correctness fix in this region: *resting* heart rate
isn't computed by averaging the raw `heart_rate` table. That gives
you 110 bpm because it includes everything you did with your heart
that day. The dashboard reads `resting_heart_rate` from Apple Health's
own daily-min aggregation, with a fallback to the mean of
`sleep_sessions.lowest_hr` over the last seven nights when that's
not available. *Resting* was always going to mean
*the floor of the day*, not *the average of the day*.

</Section>

## Tunnel reliability

<Section id="tunnel" eyebrow="WAR STORY 03" heading="My ISP, Cloudflare, and an MTU of 1280.">

The dashboard is exposed at `health.kartikp.com` via a Cloudflare
tunnel from an HP EliteDesk in my apartment. For the first month it
threw intermittent 502s that looked, *strongly*, like the application
crashing. It wasn't. The path MTU between my router and Cloudflare's
edge is roughly 1318 bytes, and the default 1500-byte MTU on `eno1`
was producing silent fragmentation that Cloudflare just gave up on
after a timeout.

The fix is two boring lines of config — `protocol: http2`,
`mtu: 1280` — plus a watchdog container I now think every
self-hosted tunnel deserves:

<Callout>
A small alpine + curl container pings Google and Cloudflare every
30 seconds. When it sees a Cloudflare-side failure pattern that
lasts ~2 minutes, it restarts `cloudflared` and pushes a
notification via ntfy.sh. I find out the tunnel was stuck before
anyone using it does. The container is twelve lines of bash.
</Callout>

<Figure
  src="/assets/work/health-dashboard/tunnel-watchdog.png"
  alt="ntfy push notification: tunnel restart event with timestamp and outage duration"
  caption="An ntfy push from the watchdog. I get a notification on my phone when cloudflared has been restarted, including how long the tunnel was stuck."
/>

The other adjacent failure mode in this story: Apple Health's
*Auto Health Export* webhook sometimes posts payloads big enough that
processing them inside the request takes more than 30 seconds — which
is the Cloudflare tunnel's origin timeout. Webhooks would time out,
the iPhone would retry, and the same payload would arrive again and
again. The fix was to respond `202 Accepted` immediately and process
the payload in a background task. If the background task fails, the
next 30-minute payload contains the same data anyway, so nothing is
lost. There is also a binary-search recovery for truncated JSON
payloads, which I would rather not have had to write.

</Section>

## Open questions

<Section id="tradeoffs" eyebrow="TRADEOFFS" heading="What I'm sitting with.">

A few decisions I haven't resolved, listed roughly in order of how
much they nag me:

- **Insights vs. surveillance.** Every metric is descriptive today.
  *"Your HRV dropped 18% this week and it correlates with your new
  magnesium glycinate"* is the obvious next step, and an LLM is the
  obvious tool. But sending health data to a hosted model has real
  privacy cost. The honest options are a local model (slower, lower
  quality), OpenAI with PII redaction (still uncomfortable), or
  refusing the upgrade. I keep refusing the upgrade.
- **Collections vs. proper schema.** Manual data — labs, supplements,
  conditions, medications — lives in a flexible JSONB
  `collection_entries` table. That's how I can spin up a new tracker
  in thirty seconds without writing a migration. It's also why labs
  can't be foreign-keyed to anything and why per-field indexing is
  unavailable. The heavy users (labs, supplements) probably want real
  tables. I haven't been able to give up the *thirty-second new
  tracker* property.
- **No caching.** Every dashboard load re-aggregates from the raw
  hypertables. At one user, that's instant. At two users on different
  pages, the database becomes the bottleneck.
  TimescaleDB continuous aggregates are the natural answer; I haven't
  needed them.
- **Single-tenant by design.** Sharing this would require row-level
  security, a real auth story, and a whole new threat model. I don't
  want to build that. The alternative — *I made the thing that lets
  me ask the questions, you can make yours* — is fine, if slightly
  unfriendly.
- **The 3D skeleton.** Beautiful, fun, debatable utility. The classic
  card view exists for the days I actually want to read numbers, and
  some days that's most of them.
- **The self-hosting tax.** I now own a tunnel, a watchdog, an
  upgrade cadence, and a backup story. The price of the data being
  mine is that I'm the one paged when something breaks. Worth it; not
  free.

</Section>
